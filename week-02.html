Week 2 · September 6, 2026

Cool Does Not Mean Useful

Key Learning Moments

This week went deeper into the AI tools themselves. Instead of just knowing that different tools exist, we looked at what each one is actually good at, and the professor walked through them using cards, which made the comparison easier to see. We also talked about outreach and customer research, meaning how you actually find the people who would use what you build.

The part that stayed with me was about the Nittany AI Challenge. We looked at what problems past teams were solving, and the lesson was simple: having something that is cool does not mean it solves a problem. It has to actually help someone. The winning teams made this obvious in their pitches. In the first five to ten seconds they said exactly what problem they were solving, and within fifteen seconds you knew how. If you can do that, the pitch is basically done. If you cannot, the rest does not matter much.

Personal Connections

This hit home because I am planning to enter the Challenge, and I was doing the exact thing the professor warned about. My first version of the idea was broad. I wanted to build a general AI companion engine that could become anything for anyone. It sounded impressive, but I could not have said in ten seconds what problem it solved.

I have now narrowed it to one problem: a voice companion for older parents in India whose kids live abroad, so the family knows how they are doing. That is something I can explain in one sentence. The bigger idea is still there, but it is the roadmap, not the pitch.

Challenges and Growth

The honest challenge is that the technical part is not what I am missing. I have built things before. What I have not done is the part this week was about: talking to actual customers before building. I have not done a single customer interview yet, and I caught myself wanting to skip that step and go straight to code. This week made it clear that is backwards. I am planning to go to the customer discovery workshop and talk to real families before I write anything serious.

AI Tool Documentation

I used Claude Code this week to research the Challenge, including the five pillars, the deadlines, and what last year's winners built, and to think through my own idea. It was useful for pressure testing the idea, but it got one thing wrong that fits this week's topic. The moment I said I liked an idea, it started writing code for it. I stopped it and told it we were still in the discussion stage. The tool treated excitement as a green light to build, and I had to be the one to hold it back. I also had it check a model's license against the actual source instead of trusting what it remembered.
