Week 8 · October 18, 2026

Patterns, Not Products

Key Learning Moments

This week was about connecting AI tools together. The professor gave us six ways to do it, simplest to most powerful: AI as a function with structured output, a grounded assistant with your documents, workflow automation like n8n, connectors through MCP, your own app calling an API, and agents. His rule was to climb only as high as your MVP needs, and most good MVPs live on the first three rungs.

The line that stuck was "learn patterns, not products." He went through tools that changed or died in the last year alone, and the point was that what lasts is how data moves, where AI adds judgment versus where rules are enough, what it costs per user, and where a human has to approve. He also showed a past student project, a syllabus date extractor built in n8n with no front end at all, and said that counts as a fully legitimate MVP.

Personal Connections

I use connectors and agents daily, so the risks he listed read like things I have already done wrong. The useful part was "choose your rung." My product sounded like it needed an agent. It does not. A daily call to a parent is a trigger, an AI step, and an action. That is rung three. The conversation is the only part that needs real AI. Everything around it is rules.

Challenges and Growth

The permissions card he had us fill in was the uncomfortable one: reads, writes, approval, log, cap. My product reads a private conversation with an old person and writes a report about them to someone else. I designed a trust screen in Week 6, but I never wrote down what the system may send without the parent knowing. That card comes before any building.

AI Tool Documentation

I talked this reflection out with voice to text and used Claude to organize it into sections. What it got wrong this week was not a name but the whole shape. The first draft came back at almost five hundred words and read like a summary of the entire lecture, every rung and every tool, with my own part squeezed into one paragraph at the end. It was organized, and it was not a reflection. I had it cut by a third and lead with the two things that actually changed how I think about my product. Same lesson every week: the output looks fine, and you still have to check it against what you meant.
